The future of work is contested across at least two registers: the macroeconomic question of which jobs survive automation, and the organizational question of how workplaces adapt to the humans who remain. Both are live problems with sources pulling in different directions.

On the macroeconomic side, [Kevin Drum's 2013 piece](/reading/2026-05/2026-05-28t074225-welcome-robot-overlords-please-dont-fire-us) laid out a structural argument: unlike past automation waves, intelligent machines are likely to displace entire labor categories rather than shift work to adjacent sectors. That thesis has aged into urgency. [Falk and Tsoukalas](/reading/2026-05/2026-05-02t155432-cognitive-offloading-and-ai-how-reliance-on-llms-affects) formalize the mechanism: competitive pressure creates a strategic trap where firms lay off workers prematurely, before automation's productivity gains are confirmed, producing collectively worse outcomes even when no individual firm is acting irrationally.

Inside organizations, the transition is already reshaping roles. [Ethan Mollick's hands-on report with Claude 5](/reading/2026-06/2026-06-09t190614-what-it-feels-like-to-work-with-mythos) finds the human function shifting from doing to commissioning, as agentic systems handle multi-hour autonomous workflows. [Werner Vogels](/reading/2026-06/2026-06-30t173037-a-return-to-two-pizza-culture) sees this compression as cause to restructure even established product processes, building prototypes before writing documents. Meanwhile the "builder versus keeper" split [Anton Zaides documents](/reading/2026-07/2026-07-07t170607-the-software-engineering-war) suggests the change is as much cultural as technical.

What persists is harder to automate. [cekrem's piece on tacit knowledge](/reading/2026-05/2026-05-19t110710-the-tacit-dimension-why-your-best-engineers-cant-tell-you) argues that pattern recognition, design intuition, and unwritten conventions are structurally inaccessible to AI and transfer only through apprenticeship. [Ghost in the Data](/reading/2026-06/2026-06-17t124905-the-competitive-moat-that-ai-cant-replicate) makes a parallel case for organizational trust: automating away human connection destroys something no personalization engine can rebuild.

Organizations are also failing at preserving human capacity through dysfunction unrelated to AI. [Poor onboarding](/reading/2026-05/2026-05-08t112608-your-onboarding-is-a-hazing-ritual-and-you-call-it-agile) systematically destroys new hires before they can contribute. [Tech hiring's error asymmetry](/reading/2026-06/2026-06-22t182141-the-systemic-decay-of-tech-hiring) selects against candidates it most needs. On-call systems that ignore finite human attention produce burnout structurally rather than incidentally, as [Abby Malson argues](/reading/2026-05/2026-05-19t134831-finite-attention-why-burnout-isnt-your-fault-and-how).

Across these threads, a consistent problem emerges: organizations optimize for measurable outputs while eroding the conditions that produce durable, skilled, trusted human work.
